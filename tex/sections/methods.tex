\section{Method}

\subsection{Substrate, population and ground truth}

Every result in this paper is measured on one substrate: HumanEval+ and MBPP+ (EvalPlus),
Python functions whose median task is 41 words of prose and 6 lines of reference code, each with
an executable hidden test suite, so every stratum, recall denominator and false-positive
population is decided by an interpreter and no model judges a recall or false-positive
outcome. The one outcome defined by adjudication, correct diagnosis in the clarification study,
is judged jointly by an author and a model, and is labelled so where it appears. The benchmark's base
suite is shown to the generator as the \emph{visible} suite and its plus suite is held back as
the \emph{hidden} one. A second substrate, the standard-library half of BigCodeBench, was built
and audited and closed without a quotable number, when its preregistered criterion was not met
on a third run; nothing from it is reported here.

\textbf{How the population was built.} The corpus has 542 problems; two, \texttt{HumanEval/32}
and \texttt{Mbpp/590}, were excluded by name before generation because their own reference
solutions fail their own hidden suites in this harness, which leaves 540. One frozen generator,
\texttt{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001}, was shown each specification and its visible suite and wrote
one solution per problem. A candidate that passes the visible suite and fails the hidden one is
a \textbf{defective} instance; one that passes both we call \textbf{correct}; one that fails the
visible suite is discarded from every recall and false-positive population. The defect stratum
is small --- about 10\% of solutions --- so, under a rule registered before the second pass and
depending only on execution outcomes, generation was repeated over the same 540 problems until
there were at least 100 defective instances or three passes. It stopped at two: 56 and 54
defective, 455 and 455 correct, 29 and 31 discarded. An instance is therefore a (pass, problem)
pair, and one problem can contribute two. Recall is measured on all \textbf{110} defective
instances, which come from \textbf{56 problems}; false positives on \textbf{150} of the 910
correct instances, drawn once from a fixed seed before any auditor ran, from 143 problems. Seven
of the 110 defective instances fail by not terminating rather than by a failed assertion, and
the registered population keeps them.

Two things follow from those definitions and are meant literally throughout. \emph{Correct} means
\emph{passes the hidden suite}: it is a benchmark label produced by an interpreter, not a
guarantee that the program meets its specification, and a suite can be incomplete or wrong. Since
this paper's own third result is that a specification often fails to determine what the hidden
suite asks, we cannot also treat passing that suite as correctness in the ordinary sense, and a
\emph{false positive} is likewise a flag on test-passing code rather than a demonstrated mistake
by the auditor. And the defect population is not a sample of model-written code: it is the
\textbf{residue that already survived the visible tests}, which is by construction the harder
part. A recall figure here is the fraction of that residue an auditor catches, never the fraction
of all defects a model writes.

\subsection{The auditor and the estimand}

The auditor reads the specification, the visible tests and the candidate, and returns findings
with severities; an increment is blocked when any finding is graded BLOCKER. It never sees the
hidden suite. The estimand throughout is \textbf{union recall at $K$}: the fraction of defective
instances blocked by at least one of $K$ independent readings, averaged exactly over all
$\binom{K_{\max}}{K}$ subsets rather than by sampling. The matching quantity on the correct
stratum is the union false-positive rate. A union recall figure is reported only beside the
false-positive rate it cost.

Four auditor routes appear below. The \textbf{shipped cross-vendor} auditor is
\texttt{gpt-5.6-terra}; the \textbf{same-vendor} auditor is \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5}, the
generator's own family and size; the \textbf{stronger same-vendor} auditor is
\texttt{claude-sonnet-4.6}; and a third family, \texttt{gpt-6-astra} at high reasoning, is read
at $K=4$. The auditor's constitution, its prompt
builder and every route's capability card are in the auditor harness, a separate repository,
at the commit named in the provenance file, whose per-reading records carry the hash of every
prompt actually sent.

The routes are \textbf{not sampled alike}, and this bounds every cross-family union-of-$K$
contrast in the paper. The cross-vendor route's capability card does not permit a sampling
temperature, so no sampling parameter is sent and the route varies between readings; the
same-vendor routes permit one and the layer sends temperature $0$, so those routes are
near-deterministic. A union over readings that do not differ cannot grow, so the two families'
unions are not equally free to rise with $K$, and a cross-family difference in union recall
confounds auditing ability with how much each union was permitted to grow. The asymmetry is a
property of the shipped routing layer we measured rather than a design choice, we did not discover
it until after the runs, and we report the $K=1$ contrast beside the $K=8$ contrast wherever it
matters. A temperature-matched replication is the first item in \S\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{Statistics}
\label{sec:stats}

The unit of observation is the instance and the resampling unit is the problem, because problems
recur across generation batches and instances sharing a problem are not independent. The $K$
readings of an instance are repeated measurements of it, not $K$ further independent observations.
The bootstrap over problems estimates uncertainty from which tasks the benchmark happens to
contain; it does not estimate how much a fresh batch of model calls on these same tasks would
move the numbers, which would need replicate runs we did not budget for at every arm. Every rate carries a 95\% percentile bootstrap over whole problem
clusters (10{,}000 resamples, seeds fixed in each preregistration), with the Wilson interval
printed beside it and marked as too narrow. Paired contrasts carry an exact McNemar test and a
cluster-level sign-flip permutation test; where every discordant pair points one way the
percentile bootstrap's bound at zero is an artefact and an unconditional interval (grid-approximated,
unclustered) is quoted
instead --- with one exception: the clarification study's registration fixed the bootstrap for a
contrast that turned out one-signed, so its interval is reported as registered and read beside
its exact cluster test (\S\ref{sec:inreview}). A saturation curve is summarised by a constrained exponential fit, and the fitted
asymptote is quoted only when the preregistered flattening bar is met; otherwise it is labelled an
extrapolation and the raw union at $K_{\max}$ is the number quoted. Counts of five or fewer are
quoted as counts. Multiplicity is handled per study as each registered it: the study carrying the
primary contrasts corrects over every comparison it computed, a Bonferroni family of sixteen with
a threshold of $0.00313$ that two comparisons clear; others registered a single primary, and
study 19 reports its twenty further quantities without correction. \textbf{Interval coverage is
measured rather than assumed or waved at.} Under the two strata's actual cluster profiles --- 110
instances in 56 problems, and 150 in 143 --- with within-problem concordance $0.5$, the
percentile bootstrap's coverage of a nominal $95\%$ rate interval is, over 400 replicates of 600
resamples each (Monte Carlo standard error about 0.011 near 95\% coverage and 0.020 at the lowest
cell; single rates only, with integer-index percentiles where the analyses interpolate; \texttt{analysis/coverage\_simulation.py}):

\noindent In these simulations coverage is near nominal at the true rates this paper's headlines
sit at and \textbf{badly anticonservative at small true rates}: at a true rate of $0.03$ on the
defect stratum the simulated interval covers about $0.80$, not $0.95$. This is evidence of
potential undercoverage for analogous intervals, not a measured coverage of any interval quoted
here. Intervals on rates below roughly $0.10$ should be read
as narrower than they claim. \emph{An earlier version of this paper said the bootstrap
under-covers ``by roughly 2 to 5 points'' and that its coverage under the clustered design was
unvalidated. The first was a generalisation the source report had already withdrawn as
unsupported; the second was wrong, since a clustered simulation sat in this paper's own test
suite. A pre-submission referee found both.} The simulation is
\texttt{analysis/coverage\_simulation.py} and costs no model calls. Two earlier paired-contrast
constructions in this programme were found by review to cover $0.416$ and $0.075$ where $0.95$
was claimed, and both were replaced. No sample size was
justified in advance by a minimum-detectable-effect calculation.

\textbf{An interval that contains zero is reported as one that does not exclude zero, and
nothing more.} No equivalence margin was registered anywhere in this programme and no
equivalence test was performed, so none of those intervals is evidence that two conditions are
alike or that an intervention did nothing --- a distinction this work has already had to make
the hard way, having once inferred equal cost from an interval containing zero and withdrawn it
on review. Where a hypothesis fails its registered criterion we say it failed, not that the
effect is absent.

All analysis is plain Python 3.13 using only the standard library --- \texttt{random},
\texttt{math}, \texttt{itertools}, \texttt{csv} and \texttt{json}. The bootstrap, the exact
McNemar test, the cluster sign-flip permutation and the exact subset enumeration behind every
union-at-$K$ figure are implemented in the repository rather than called from a numerical
package, so each is readable at the point where it is used, and none depends on a third-party
version.

\subsection{Preregistration and independent review}
\label{sec:methods-review}

Each study that made model calls was preregistered before its first one, fixing its population,
hypotheses, primary outcome, intervals, kill conditions, budget and stopping rule ---
\textbf{with two exceptions this paper reports rather than elides}. First, the severity sweep of
\S\ref{sec:auditor} re-grades findings already archived, makes no model calls, and was never
registered: every figure it produces is post hoc and exploratory, and none is a confirmatory
test. An earlier version of
this paragraph named only the second exception. Second, the analysis behind
\S\ref{sec:inreview}'s third-rating result was registered a day \emph{after} its contrast had
been computed and committed, in a document that claimed the opposite; that document is withdrawn
in the repository and the analysis is registered nowhere. Its method was fixed in advance by an
earlier amendment, and the disjoint reading, the abstention-excluded secondary and the
broken-sheet comparison were not. Departures are numbered
amendments committed before the step they govern, each recording what was known at the time; where
an amendment followed an interim observation, the results say so and argue whether the change
could favour the hypothesis. Each report was then reviewed by a model from a different vendor to the generator, with
read access to the raw records, asked to re-run the analysis, recompute the intervals
independently, and check every sentence against the records. \textbf{That reviewer is
\texttt{gpt-6-astra}, which is also one of the auditor families measured in \S\ref{sec:auditor}
and one of the two raters behind \S\ref{sec:residual}'s labels, and one of the two
adjudicators whose joint labels define the clarification study's outcome.} For most claims this is a
cross-vendor check on work the reviewer had no part in. \textbf{For the claims that rank
\texttt{astra} against the other families it is not: there the reviewer is the subject}, and
those comparisons, and the clarification study's adjudication, should be read as
self-assessed until a model that audited nothing in this
programme has checked them. This project's own P3 Amendment 10 disqualified the same model as the
clarification study's manipulation-check rater for the same reason, yet kept it as that study's
second adjudicator and its reviewer; the standard was applied unevenly within one study, and not
applied to the review at all until a pre-submission referee pointed it out. Reviews are archived verbatim, including those that
refused approval, and several studies needed many rounds: on one day five reports were refused
at once, and clearing those five took thirty-one further rounds. What moved, in most of them,
was what the reports claimed about their measurements --- and in most of the later refusals the object of the refusal was a repair written to satisfy the previous
round, not the study it repaired. We state that because it is the most transferable thing this
process produced: a fix written to satisfy a reviewer is itself an unreviewed claim.

\textbf{The reviewer is a language model, and this paper is about the ceiling on language
models reading code they did not write.} That applies to our own instrument, and the round
counts are the evidence: the studies here cleared review at rounds \textbf{3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10 and
11}, and in every case the round before the last still returned substantive findings. Some of those
findings were defects introduced by the repairs between rounds, so the counts do not estimate a
single pass's recall; they show that no report cleared in fewer than three. \emph{Quotable at
round $N$} means a report survived $N$ passes, not that it was verified, and the marks in this paper
should be read as a filter of unknown recall rather than as proof.

Two things sharpen that. First, the split. Measurements did move on review: the severity
sweep's first review found its loader misreading one family's draws and another's population,
and every cross-family figure was recomputed; the clarification study's second review moved its primary
from 9 of 31 to 9 of 32 instances and its contrast from $+29.0$ to $+28.1$ points, by finding
an instance wrongly excluded; and a label count inside a reviewed report was 71 where its data
say 67. An earlier version of this sentence said measurements never moved, and a later one
confined the exceptions to one study; both were wrong. Once a figure's inputs were right, the
reviewers reproduced rates, intervals, subset enumerations and seeds independently and found no
arithmetic error, and most refusals were about what the prose claimed. On recomputation the instrument is strong; on
interpretation it is weak, and the two should not be quoted as one assurance. Second, the class
of defect this leaves open is the one that looks right to a language model. The clarification
study audited the wrong program for 384 readings --- a per-problem field used where a
per-instance one was needed --- and every automated gate passed, because each checked that the
code was \emph{identical across conditions} and it was, identically wrong. It was found by hand,
reading an adjudication sheet and noticing that a displayed program returned exactly what its
hidden suite was said to reject. Neither the gates nor a reviewer found it, and we do not know
what else of that kind remains.

\textbf{Every review prompt in this programme stated the claimed result before the reviewer
began.} We attempted to test what that costs, and the attempt failed in a way worth reporting.
One report, quotable at round 6 after five refusals and unchanged since, was dispatched again
under a prompt whose own text withheld every figure and every prior verdict and asked for an
independent computation first. \textbf{That prompt's list of binding artefacts then named two
files that state those figures}, one of them the claims-ledger entry for the result under
review. The blinding was not enforced, so the agreement between that pass and the briefed one
establishes nothing, and we draw no inference from it. What the pass did return is a
\emph{disagreement count}: six substantive findings that six briefed rounds on the same file had
not produced, among them that the report's two groups were two different definitions of
\emph{caught} rather than one population with duplicate rows, that its rating sheet displays at
most three failing inputs while 81 of its 121 entries have more recorded, and that a count of
changed labels was 71 where the data say 67. **That count is $n=1$, uncontrolled and
unregistered**: a seventh round might have found new things under any prompt, and no
matched briefed round was run against the same file. We report it as a reason to test blinding
properly rather than as a result about blinding, and the first round of every review after this
one is dispatched with the conclusions withheld from the prompt \emph{and} from every artefact
it names.

Every study drawn on here has completed that review. One further study was re-run twice and closed
without a quotable number; the paper names it only to say what it no longer supports.
