\section{Introduction}
When a model writes code, reports a result or prepares a dataset, someone has to check it, and
increasingly that someone is another model. CrossAudit, the system every measurement here passes
through (\S\ref{sec:system}), is built on two rules for doing that. The model that audits must come
from a different vendor than the model that wrote the work, and what a deterministic check can verify
is verified by code, not by a model's opinion. An automated auditor of this kind reads work it did not
write and says whether to block it. We ask how much it catches, what decides the limit, and whether
the arrangement helps where checking matters most, in science.

A reviewer's score on a benchmark mixes at least four things: whether the model can see a defect,
whether it judges the defect worth blocking on, whether the benchmark's hidden tests define the
behaviour it would have to see, and how many times it is allowed to look. Work on code-review and
critic models improves the first two and reports the aggregate gain. We hold the auditor, its
instructions and its ground truth fixed and vary the other factors one at a time, against a truth
no model supplies: a hidden test suite executed by an interpreter. Because each factor is varied
alone, we measure how far each moves the limit and do not apportion the limit among them.

Returns to reading again diminish. Eight independent readings of the same increment by the
shipped cross-vendor auditor reach 30.0\% union recall on defective increments, with a 95\%
problem-cluster interval of 20.0 to 40.7, at 16.0\% [10.1, 22.3] on correct ones. The eighth
reading still adds 1.9 points [1.1, 2.8], above the 1.0-point flattening bar we registered in
advance, so the curve had not flattened at the budget we could afford and we do not claim
saturation. Twenty readings pooled across three auditor families reach 48.2\%, at 36.0\% false
positives on correct code.

Which model reads, and what it is told to look for, also move the limit. Swapping in a stronger
model of the generator's own vendor changes union recall by -26.4 points [-37.3, -15.6], a
preregistered primary. That contrast does not survive its own controls. The stronger model flags
correct code at 3.3\% against 16.0\%, the sign reverses under a weaker severity rule, and the two
routes are sampled differently (one at temperature 0, one with no sampling parameter sent), so
their unions are not equally free to grow. A third model reaches higher observed recall than the
shipped auditor at fewer false positives with half the readings, though no paired contrast
establishes both advantages at once.

Adding one sentence to the auditor's rulebook, telling it to look for what the visible tests do
not cover, moves flags by +26.8 points [13.6, 40.4] on a 56-instance exploratory arm. On correct
code it adds 12.5 points of flags, a cost whose admissible interval, $[-3.6, +26.8]$, does not
exclude zero; under the rule that stratum is flagged at 10 of 56, above the product's 6.7\%
false-positive constraint as an observed rate, against 3 of 56 without it. On the contrast that
isolates the sentence, hidden-suite pass rate moves +5.4 points [-0.9, 12.1], which also does not
exclude zero.

Much of what the auditor misses is failure the specification never determined. We re-rated the
never-flagged residual under a rule that asks whether the prose settles the hidden suite's
expected value before it asks whether the visible tests exercised the input. Two raters agree that
44 of 57 residual instances are cases where the prose does not settle it, reversing the same
instances' earlier classification. Where an outside party had already flagged one of these
specifications as defective, for their own purposes and before we looked, our raters agree on 9 of
the 11 tasks that overlap.

We tried to test that prospectively, by constructing a population whose
specification-determinedness would hold by construction, and the attempt failed. Independent
review found that our construction never enforced the property. The filter registered to recover
the failing input is a truthiness check on a field the injector wrote: of 281 recorded witnesses,
179 are strings, 102 are lists, and none is executed. Four of the other five filters accept cases
their registered wording excludes. What the population actually contains is small injected edits
that survive a sparse visible suite and fail a hidden one. A preregistered probe separates those
edits from natural code at 96.7\% on the items it answered, \emph{by a feature it does not
identify}: its two arms share no problem, so task composition alone could produce the result. The
prospective test therefore does not exist yet, and the classification above remains post hoc.

A union recall figure is contingent on the false-positive rate it cost, so the ceiling we measure
describes an operating point rather than a constant. We make that argument on methodological
grounds rather than from a second measurement. We attempted one, on a substrate with much longer
specifications, and it closed without a quotable number after three runs
(\S\ref{sec:inreview}): across three extractions of the same protocol, whether the two
substrates' false-positive intervals meet came out apart, overlapping, and apart again.

Then we take the same system into science (\S\ref{sec:ai4s}), with one preregistered study for
each job a scientist would hand an auditor, and ground truth no model supplies. On reported results,
the deterministic check that every number traces to its source caught every inconsistency among a
report, its results file and its run log, and none of 25 results that were wrong consistently in all
three. The model auditor, recomputing from the code, flagged 23 of those 25 (21 with a sound finding,
post hoc). On data, a validator written from the dataset's documentation and the shipped auditor
caught different faults, 92 and 65 of 140, and together 112, at the auditor's 2.9\% flag rate on
clean data. Neither tier replaces the other: the deterministic one had no false positives and could
not see consistent error, while the model saw some of it and made mistakes of its own. On scientific code, with
no tests shown, the auditor's flag rate moved substantially with what the task named as the
deliverable: naming the step rather than the whole problem cut its flags on correct code from 68.7\%
to 36.0\% and its recall from 87.7\% to 72.8\%, on the same programs. In all three studies the
auditor also flagged defects in our own construction of items meant to be clean, which is itself a
finding about how an auditor should be evaluated.

The paper follows that order. Section~\ref{sec:system} describes the system. Sections~\ref{sec:method}
and~\ref{sec:results} measure its model tier's limit on general-purpose code and what moves it.
Section~\ref{sec:ai4s} tests the two tiers on scientific code, results and data.

\subsection{Preregistration and review}

Every study that made model calls was preregistered before its first one, with hypotheses,
intervals, kill conditions and stopping rules fixed in advance. Planned departures were recorded
as numbered amendments saying what was known at the time. Deviations found only afterwards, such
as checks implemented more narrowly than registered or spend guards weaker than registered, are
disclosed in the reports rather than amended in advance. Two analyses were not registered in
advance, and the paper names both: a re-grading of archived findings, which was never registered,
and a third rating whose registration came after its result and is withdrawn. Four preregistered
hypotheses were killed by their own rules, two of them our own conjectures.

Each report was reviewed by a model from a different vendor with access to the raw records, and
the reviews are archived verbatim beside the results they judge. Every study reported here has
completed that review, at between three and twenty-one rounds each; Section~\ref{sec:inreview}
collects the eight that finished after this paper was first drafted. That record is what lets the
negative results here be read as measurements.
