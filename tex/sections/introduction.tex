\section{Introduction}
Here is a program that none of twenty readings, by three different auditor models, flagged. The
task,
MBPP+ problem 102 \citep{liu2023evalplus}, asks for a function that converts a snake-case string to
camel case, and shows three ordinary examples. The generated function splits on underscores and
capitalises each word, and it passes all three. The hidden suite then passes
\verb|___python_program| and expects \verb|___PythonProgram|; the function returns
\verb|PythonProgram|. The task does not say how leading underscores should be handled. Both model
raters in a post hoc assessment classified this instance as undetermined by the prose, and it still
counts as defective under our hidden-test definition.

When a model writes code, reports a result or prepares a dataset, someone has to check it, and
increasingly that someone is another model. This paper measures how often such a checker flags
defective work, what moves that rate, and whether it helps where checking matters most, in science.
We study one shipped system, CrossAudit (Section~\ref{sec:system}), through which every auditor
reading here passes. It rests on two rules: the model that audits comes from a different vendor from
the model that wrote the work, and whatever a deterministic check can verify is verified by code, not
by a model's opinion. The shipped configuration pairs different vendors; our comparisons also
include same-vendor controls.

An auditor's score on a benchmark combines at least four things: whether it flags a program, which
of its findings count as blocking, how many times it is asked, and whether the expected behaviour
follows from the specification it is shown. Work on critic models usually reports the aggregate. We
vary these factors one at a time where the design allows, against a truth no model supplies: a
hidden test suite run by an interpreter. Two limits apply throughout. Changing the reading model also
changed its sampling, so route comparisons do not isolate model ability. And the role of the
specification is a post hoc association, not a tested cause.

\textbf{General-purpose code} (Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}). At least one of eight independent
readings of the shipped auditor flags 30.0\% [20.0, 40.7] of defective programs that pass their
visible tests, and 16.0\% [10.1, 22.3] of correct ones. Each extra reading adds less, but the eighth
still adds 1.93 points [1.14, 2.78]: no plateau within eight readings. By the \emph{ceiling} of a configuration we therefore mean the union recall it reaches within the readings we could afford, at the false-positive rate that cost, not an asymptote, which would be an extrapolation. The rate also changes with the
auditor route, its instructions and the counting rule. What no reading flags mostly resembles the
example above: of the 57 defective programs none of those twenty readings flagged,
two model raters judge that in 44 (77.2\% [62.1, 91.1], post hoc) the prose does not entail the value the hidden suite expects on the first failing input: it is silent, open to the program's reading, or contradicts the suite.

\textbf{Science} (Section~\ref{sec:ai4s}). Three preregistered studies examine the jobs a scientist
would hand an auditor: scientific code, reported results and data. The results and data studies
compare model auditing with deterministic verification, and the two failed in different places.
Checking that every reported number traces to its source flagged every inconsistency among a report,
its results file and its run log, and none of the results that were wrong consistently in all three;
the model auditor flagged nearly all of those. On data, a validator written from the documentation
and the model flagged overlapping sets of faulty items, so together they covered more than either. On
scientific code, with no tests shown, a task reframing that specified the step as the deliverable,
moved the overall problem into context and exempted earlier functions sharply reduced the auditor's
flags on correct programs, and less sharply on defective ones.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item A way to measure an automated auditor's limit against executed ground truth, with union recall
  always reported beside the false-positive rate it cost: controlled changes to the number of
  readings and the rulebook, route comparisons that change model and sampling together, re-grading
  under decision rules, and a post hoc association with what the specification determines. On
  general-purpose code, each controlled or route contrast moves the flag rate, so a recall figure
  without its operating point does not transfer; the specification analysis describes an
  association, not a tested cause.
\item Operating points of the model auditor on scientific code, and comparisons of the model tier
  with deterministic verification on reported results (the shipped provenance checks and
  re-execution) and on data (a documentation-derived validator). The tiers flag different faults,
  and each fails where the other does not.
\item A lesson for evaluating auditors. In the results and data studies, reading every flag on a
  clean item showed the auditor finding real defects in our own construction of those items. An
  evaluation must establish that its clean items are clean by the auditor's standard; otherwise its
  apparent false-positive rate can include valid findings about defects in the evaluation materials.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Preregistration and review.} Every study that made model calls was preregistered before its
first call, with hypotheses, intervals and stopping rules fixed in advance and departures recorded as
numbered amendments. Two whole analyses were not registered, and the paper names both
(Appendix~\ref{sec:method}); every other unregistered quantity is labelled post hoc where it appears.
Five preregistered hypotheses met their own rejection criteria, one of them in the science studies.
Every report was reviewed against the raw records by a model from a different vendor, and the reviews
are archived verbatim (Appendix~\ref{sec:method}). Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what the
measurements mean. The full method, results and discussion are in
Appendices~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:discussion-full}, and the records, reviews and the command that
regenerates every figure are described in Appendix~\ref{sec:repro}.
