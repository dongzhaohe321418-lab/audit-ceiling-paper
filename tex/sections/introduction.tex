\section{Introduction}
Here is a program that no reading of our first three auditor families flagged. The task, from MBPP+
\citep{liu2023evalplus}, asks for a function that converts a snake-case string to camel case, and
shows three ordinary examples. The generated function splits on underscores and capitalises each
word, and it passes all three. The hidden suite then passes \verb|___python_program| and
expects \verb|___PythonProgram|: the leading underscores kept, the rest converted. The task
never says so. Whoever reads this code against this task has little to object to, and the defect
is real only because a test suite nobody showed the reader decides what is correct.

When a model writes code, reports a result or prepares a dataset, someone has to check it, and
increasingly that someone is another model. This paper measures how much such a checker catches,
what sets its limit, and whether it helps where checking matters most, in science. We study one
shipped system, CrossAudit (Section~\ref{sec:system}), through which every auditor reading here
passes. It rests on two rules: the model that audits comes from a different vendor from the model
that wrote the work, and whatever a deterministic check can verify is verified by code, not by a
model's opinion.

A reviewer's score on a benchmark mixes at least four things: whether the model can see a defect,
whether it judges the defect worth blocking on, whether the benchmark defines the behaviour it
would have to see, and how many times it looks. Work on critic models usually reports the
aggregate. We vary these factors one at a time where the design allows, against a truth no model
supplies: a hidden test suite run by an interpreter. Some contrasts cannot be isolated. Changing the
reading model also changed its sampling, and the role of the specification is a post hoc
association.

\textbf{General-purpose code} (Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}). Eight independent readings of the shipped
auditor catch 30.0\% [20.0, 40.7] of defective programs that pass their visible tests, while
flagging 16.0\% [10.1, 22.3] of correct ones. Each extra reading adds less, but the eighth still
adds 1.93 points [1.14, 2.78]: no plateau within eight readings. The figure is not a property of the auditor
alone. A stronger same-vendor model, sampled differently, catches 26.4 points less
[$-37.3$, $-15.6$] at 3.3\% false positives, and under a looser any-finding rule the comparison
reverses. One added rulebook sentence raises the flag rate on defective code by 26.8 points
[13.6, 40.4] (exploratory, 56 instances, one reading), and on correct code by 12.5 points, an interval
[$-3.6$, $+26.8$] that includes zero; it does not measurably change whether code passes after
revision. And what the auditors miss mostly resembles the example above: of the 57 defective
programs no reading of the first three auditor families flagged, two model raters judge 44 (77.2\% [62.1,
91.1], post hoc) to fail on behaviour the specification never determined.

\textbf{Science} (Section~\ref{sec:ai4s}). We then ran one preregistered study for each job a
scientist would hand an auditor, and the two kinds of check failed in different places. On reported
results, the check that every number traces to its source caught all 49 inconsistencies among a
report, its results file and its run log, and none of 25 results that were wrong consistently in
all three; the model auditor flagged 23 of those 25, 21 for the right reason (post hoc). On data, a
validator written from the documentation and the auditor flagged overlapping but different faulty
items, 92 and 65 of 140, together 112, at the auditor's 2.9\% flag rate on clean data (post hoc,
counting only findings about the injected fault: 61 and 110). On scientific code, naming the step
rather than the whole problem as the task cut the auditor's flags on correct code from 68.7\% to
36.0\% and its recall from 87.7\% to 72.8\%, on the same programs.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item A way to measure an automated auditor's limit against executed ground truth, with union
  recall always reported beside the false-positive rate it cost: controlled changes to the number
  of readings and the rulebook, route comparisons that change model and sampling together,
  re-grading under decision rules, and a post hoc association with what the specification
  determines.
\item Evidence that no single factor fixes the limit on general-purpose code. Readings, rulebook,
  route and decision rule each move it, and what is missed is associated (post hoc, not isolated
  as a cause) with what the specification leaves open. A recall figure without its operating point
  does not transfer.
\item Operating points of the model auditor on scientific code, and comparisons of the model tier
  with deterministic verification on reported results (the shipped provenance checks and
  re-execution) and on data (a documentation-derived validator). The tiers flag different faults,
  and each fails where the other does not.
\item A lesson for evaluating auditors. In the results and data studies, reading every flag on a
  clean item showed the auditor finding real defects in our own construction of those items. An
  evaluation must establish that its clean items are clean by the auditor's standard, or its
  false-positive rate measures the evaluator.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Preregistration and review.} Every study that made model calls was preregistered before its
first call, with hypotheses, intervals and stopping rules fixed in advance and departures recorded
as numbered amendments. Two whole analyses were not registered, and the paper names both
(Appendix~\ref{sec:method}); every other unregistered quantity is labelled post hoc where it
appears. Four preregistered hypotheses were killed by their own rules, and one more in the science
studies. Every report was reviewed, with access to the raw records, by a model from a different
vendor, for between three and twenty-one rounds (the derived-quantities report within the
whole-manuscript review); the reviews are archived verbatim. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what the measurements
mean. The full method, results and discussion
are in Appendices~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:discussion-full}, and the records, reviews and the
command that regenerates every figure are described in Appendix~\ref{sec:repro}.
