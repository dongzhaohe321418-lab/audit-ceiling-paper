\section{Introduction}
An automated auditor reads code it did not write and says whether to block it. We ask how much
such an auditor catches, and what decides the limit.

That question decomposes. A reviewer's score on a benchmark
mixes at least four things together: whether the model can see a defect, whether it judges the
defect worth blocking on, whether the benchmark's hidden tests define the behaviour it would have
to see, and how many times it is allowed to look. Work on code-review and critic models improves
the first two and reports the aggregate gain. We hold the auditor, its instructions and its ground
truth fixed, and vary the other three one at a time, against a truth no model supplies: a hidden
test suite executed by an interpreter.

Returns to reading again diminish. Eight independent readings of the same increment by the
shipped cross-vendor auditor reach 30.0\% union recall on defective increments, with a 95\%
problem-cluster interval of 20.0 to 40.7, at 16.0\% [10.1, 22.3] on correct ones. The eighth
reading still adds 1.9 points [1.1, 2.8], above the 1.0-point flattening bar we registered in
advance, so the curve had not flattened at the budget we could afford and we do not claim
saturation. Twenty readings pooled across three auditor families reach 48.2\%.

Which model reads, and what it is told to look for, move the limit further. Swapping in a stronger
model of the generator's own vendor changes union recall by -26.4 points [-37.3, -15.6], a
preregistered primary. That contrast does not survive its own controls: the stronger model flags
correct code at 3.3\% against 16.0\%, the sign reverses under a weaker severity rule, and the two
routes are sampled differently, one at temperature 0 and one with no sampling parameter sent, so
their unions are not equally free to grow. A third model beats both on recall and false positives
at half the readings. Adding one sentence to the auditor's rulebook, telling it to look for what
the visible tests do not cover, moves flags by +26.8 points [13.6, 40.4] on a 56-instance
exploratory arm, at 12.5 points more flags on correct code --- a cost whose admissible
interval is $[-3.6, +26.8]$ and does not exclude zero, so it is the pooled contrast rather than
the correct stratum alone that puts the rule outside the product's constraint; on the contrast that isolates the sentence, hidden-suite pass rate moves
+5.4 points [-0.9, 12.1] and does not exclude zero.

Much of what the auditor misses turns out to be failure the specification never determined. We
re-rated the never-flagged residual under a rule that asks whether the prose settles the hidden
suite's expected value before it asks whether the visible tests exercised the input. Two raters
agree that 44 of 57 residual instances are cases where the prose does not settle it, reversing the
same instances' earlier classification. Where an outside party had already flagged one of these
specifications as defective, for their own purposes and before we looked, our raters agree on 9 of
the 11 tasks that overlap.

We tried to test that prospectively, by constructing a population whose specification-determinedness
would hold by construction, and the attempt failed in a way worth reporting. Independent review
established that our construction never enforced the property: the filter registered to recover the
failing input is a truthiness check on a field the injector wrote --- of 281 recorded witnesses,
179 are strings and 102 are lists, and none is executed --- and four of the other five filters
accept cases their registered wording excludes. What the population actually contains is small
injected edits that survive a sparse visible suite and fail a hidden one. A preregistered probe
separates those edits from natural code at 96.7\% on the items it answered, \emph{by a feature
it does not identify}: its two arms share no problem, so task composition alone could produce
the result. The prospective test therefore does not exist yet, and the classification above
remains post hoc.

A union recall figure is contingent on the false-positive rate it cost, so the ceiling we
measure describes an operating point rather than a constant. We argue that as a methodological
point and not from a second measurement: we attempted one on a substrate with much longer
specifications, and it closed without a quotable number after three runs
(\S\ref{sec:inreview}). Its clearest lesson is the one we do not get to quote --- across
three extractions of the same protocol, the quantity we had most wanted to use, whether the two
substrates' false-positive intervals meet, came out apart, overlapping, and apart again.

\subsection{Preregistration and review}

Every study was preregistered before its first model call, with hypotheses, intervals, kill
conditions and stopping rules fixed in advance, and every departure recorded as a numbered
amendment that says what was known at the time, with one exception the paper names and whose registration is withdrawn. Four preregistered hypotheses were killed by their
own rules, two of them our own conjectures, and one of those kills requires a headline in this
paper to be stated as a flag rate rather than a recall. Reports are reviewed by a model from a
different vendor with access to the raw records, several needing many rounds, and the reviews are
archived verbatim beside the results they judge. Every study reported here has completed that review,
at between four and eleven rounds each, and Section~\ref{sec:inreview} collects the six that
were still in it when this paper was first written. A seventh, an intervention on the
specification, has been refused twice and licenses nothing here. We record this as method, and it is what lets a reader read the negative results here
as measurements.
