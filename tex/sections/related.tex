\section{Related work}

\textbf{Critics that read code.} Trained critic models write natural-language critiques of
model-written code and outperform unaided humans on inserted bugs \citep{mcaleese2024critics}.
Two differences bear on what a ceiling means. Such work is evaluated largely on inserted defects,
which are specification-determined by construction, whereas our natural stratum is the residue of
an ordinary generation run, where whether the specification determines the failure is the open
question. And the reported quantity is a preference or a per-critique comparison rather than the
union recall of repeated independent readings, so it does not say where reading saturates.
Aggregating several review passes is known to help: multi-review aggregation on 1{,}000 verified
pull requests raises F1 by up to 43.67\% \citep{zeng2025swrbench}. That is the same mechanical
move as our union-of-$K$, used as a method rather than as an instrument for finding a limit.

\textbf{What the hidden suite decides.} EvalPlus rebuilt HumanEval and MBPP with larger suites and
reported defects in the original reference solutions \citep{liu2023evalplus}; its code carries
bespoke oracles for tasks where differential testing against the reference was not applicable.
\citet{richter2026collapse} show that models collapse onto a single incorrect reading of an
underspecified task and name twelve MBPP tasks they identified by hand; eleven fall inside our
defect population and our raters, who labelled them before that paper was fetched, agree on nine.
From the test-generation side, the dominant error in generated tests is a wrong expected value
rather than an invalid input \citep{xiong2023testing}, and generated oracles tend to capture a
program's actual rather than its intended behaviour \citep{konstantinou2024oracles}. Our claim is
the auditing counterpart: where the prose does not fix the value, a recall number computed against
that suite measures the benchmark as much as the auditor.

\textbf{Consensus as a substitute for ground truth.} Several systems validate generated tests
without a reference by agreement, through self-consistency, dual execution, or multi-agent discussion for {JUnit}
suites \citep{taherkhani2026consistency, xu2025consensus}. In neither paper could we find the quantity that decides whether such
a filter can be trusted: how many wrong assertions survive it. We measure it on a frozen set of canonical-failing tests and find that corroboration
across independent generations does not reach our preregistered threshold, because wrong tests
recur by problem; that study is \emph{in review} (\S\ref{sec:inreview}). This is the concrete case of a general result: self-consistent errors are
exactly the class that every detection method they evaluate struggles to detect
\citep{tan2025consistent}.

\textbf{Who audits whom.} That an evaluator should not be the model under evaluation is supported
by measurements of self-preference in LLM judges against verifiable ground truth
\citep{chen2025selfpreference}. Our
contribution is its size against model-free ground truth, and the finding that its direction is
substrate-dependent.

\textbf{Preregistration.} Preregistering an evaluation protocol is beginning to appear in machine
learning, both as a protocol for experiments with agents \citep{vaccaro2026prereg} and as a defence
against tuning prompts, decoding parameters or output format until a desired result appears
\citep{thomas2026phacking}. We record our use of it as
method rather than contribution.
