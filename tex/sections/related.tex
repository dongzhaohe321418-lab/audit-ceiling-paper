\section{Related work}

\textbf{Critics that read code.} Trained critic models write natural-language critiques of
model-written code and outperform unaided humans at catching bugs \citep{mcaleese2024critics}.
That work evaluates on both deliberately inserted bugs and naturally occurring ones: on code
containing naturally occurring model errors its critiques are preferred to human critiques in
63\% of cases, and it surfaces errors in training data previously rated flawless. It also reports
that critics hallucinate bugs, and that a human--model team hallucinates less than the model
alone. So the natural-defect setting and the precision cost of a louder critic are both already
its territory, and we do not claim either as new. And the reported quantity is a preference or a per-critique comparison rather than the
union recall of repeated independent readings against an executed oracle. We should be plain
that this work already occupies two of our four axes: it reports a precision--recall trade-off for
critics, and it separates tampered from natural bugs. What we add on this axis is narrow --- the
union of repeated independent readings as an instrument for locating a limit, ground truth
supplied by an interpreter rather than by preference, and the false-positive rate carried beside
every recall figure. Aggregating several review passes is known to help: multi-review aggregation
on 1{,}000 verified pull requests raises F1 by up to 43.67\% \citep{zeng2025swrbench}. That is
the same mechanical move as our union-of-$K$, used as a method rather than as an instrument for
finding a limit.

\textbf{Repeated sampling and coverage.} That coverage --- the fraction of problems solved by at
least one of $K$ samples --- rises steeply and then flattens is an established regularity of
repeated sampling, reported over four orders of magnitude of $K$ and fitted by a power law
\citep{brown2024monkeys}. Our union-of-$K$ is that quantity with a flag rule in place of a
correctness check, so \S\ref{sec:repeat} re-measures a known shape rather than discovering one,
and at $K \le 8$ our budget is small beside theirs. The contribution is not the shape. It is
that the curve is read here as a measurement of a ceiling, reported beside the false-positive rate
it costs, and tested against a registered flattening bar that it fails --- which is why we report
diminishing returns rather than a limit.

\textbf{Self-assessment.} That models are poor judges of their own output is established from two
directions: self-correction without external feedback does not reliably improve reasoning and can
degrade it \citep{huang2024selfcorrect}, and models prefer their own generations when used as
judges \citep{chen2025selfpreference}. Our same-vendor and self-audit arms are the code-audit case
of that literature, and we read our null there as consistent with it rather than as new. What our
arms add is the false-positive rate beside the recall, and the observation that the direction
reverses on a second substrate.

\textbf{What the hidden suite decides.} EvalPlus rebuilt HumanEval and MBPP with larger suites and
reported defects in the original reference solutions \citep{liu2023evalplus}; its code carries
bespoke oracles for tasks where differential testing against the reference was not applicable.
\citet{richter2026collapse} show that models collapse onto a single incorrect reading of an
underspecified task and name twelve MBPP tasks they identified by hand; eleven fall inside our
defect population and our raters, who labelled them before that paper was fetched, agree on nine.
From the test-generation side, the dominant error in generated tests is a wrong expected value
rather than an invalid input \citep{xiong2023testing}, and generated oracles tend to capture a
program's actual rather than its intended behaviour \citep{konstantinou2024oracles}. Our claim is
the auditing counterpart: where the prose does not fix the value, a recall number computed against
that suite measures the benchmark as much as the auditor.

\textbf{Consensus as a substitute for ground truth.} Several systems validate generated tests
without a reference by agreement, through self-consistency, dual execution, or multi-agent discussion for {JUnit}
suites \citep{taherkhani2026consistency, xu2025consensus}. In neither paper could we find the quantity that decides whether such
a filter can be trusted: how many wrong assertions survive it. We measure it on a frozen set of canonical-failing tests and find that corroboration
across independent generations does not reach our preregistered threshold, because wrong tests
recur by problem; that study is \emph{in review} (\S\ref{sec:inreview}). This is the concrete case of a general result: self-consistent errors are
exactly the class that every detection method they evaluate struggles to detect
\citep{tan2025consistent}.

\textbf{Who audits whom.} That an evaluator should not be the model under evaluation is supported
by measurements of self-preference in LLM judges against verifiable ground truth
\citep{chen2025selfpreference}. Our
contribution is its size against model-free ground truth, and the finding that its direction is
substrate-dependent.

\textbf{Preregistration.} Preregistering an evaluation protocol is beginning to appear in machine
learning, both as a protocol for experiments with agents \citep{vaccaro2026prereg} and as a defence
against tuning prompts, decoding parameters or output format until a desired result appears
\citep{thomas2026phacking}. We record our use of it as
method rather than contribution.
