\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What a union-recall number means}

We report the shipped auditor's union recall over eight independent readings as 30.0\% on one
substrate and 71.0\% on another, and both figures are contingent on the rate they cost. On the
second substrate the same auditor flags 55.3\% of correct work where it flagged 16.0\% on the first,
and it buys less recall per unit of false-positive cost at every reading count. A single reading on
the second substrate already costs more false positives than eight readings on the first: 31.8\%
[24.9, 39.0] against 16.0\% [10.1, 22.3], intervals that do not meet, a gap of 2.6 points. For the
cross-vendor auditor, then, no reading count gives a matched false-positive rate across the two
substrates. That holds for the cross-vendor auditor, which is the one the headline uses. Pooling
the families breaks it, because substrate 1's same-vendor auditor reaches 24.0\% [17.2, 31.2],
which overlaps substrate 2's range. An earlier draft asserted the unrestricted version on point
estimates alone, and a review of the figures caught it. The second substrate is \emph{in review}
(\S\ref{sec:inreview}).

Two limits on that argument. The two substrates differ in specification length, solution length,
problem population, defect distribution and suite construction at once, so this is a comparison of
two operating points rather than a manipulation of one. The clean version of the experiment is a
severity-threshold sweep within a single substrate, which we did not run. And non-overlap of two
separately constructed intervals is a conservative proxy for a difference test, not a test, and the
statement covers eight correlated reading counts without a correction for that.

An auditor's recall is a property of where it stands on its own trade-off, and the trade-off moves
with the material, so the two substrates measure one auditor at two operating points. Comparisons
of reviewers and critics that report recall, F1 or win rates without the corresponding rate on
correct work are comparing operating points that need not be the same. The same caution applies to
this paper's own contrasts, and \S\ref{sec:auditor} reports where it bites hardest on our results.

We do not quote precision. Precision depends on how much defective work an audit actually meets,
and our strata are built at a designed ratio --- 110 defective against 150 correct on the first
substrate --- chosen for the power of the contrasts and not to match any deployment. A precision
computed at that ratio would describe our design rather than anyone's repository. Reporting recall
beside its false-positive rate, as we do throughout, lets a reader who knows their own prevalence
compute it; reporting our precision would invite them not to.

\subsection{Repeated reading buys coverage only where the decision is marginal}

Union-of-K is the instrument this paper uses to find a ceiling, and on the second substrate the
same-vendor auditor shows what it measures when it measures nothing. That auditor's block decision
is identical across all eight independent readings on every one of 250 instances, while its reply
text, cost and latency vary. Its union curve is flat, and the registered efficiency ratio, which
divides by the gain in false positives over one reading, is undefined rather than large.

Part of that is arithmetic and needs no experiment: a union over readings that do not differ cannot
grow. Part of it is a design fact this paper had to correct for, which is that the same-vendor
route is sent temperature $0$ while the cross-vendor route is sent no sampling parameter at all
(\S\ref{sec:auditor}). What the data add beyond those two is the operating point. On the second
substrate that auditor flags 88.0\% [78.0, 96.0] of defective and 77.3\% [69.3, 84.9] of correct
increments, so almost nothing sits near its decision boundary and resampling has little left to
flip. The cross-vendor auditor on the same substrate, at 71.0\% and 55.3\%, splits its verdict
across draws on 103 of 250 instances; the same-vendor auditor splits on none. We cannot separate
the two explanations with the runs we have, because the arm that is flat is also the arm sampled
at temperature 0, and no temperature-matched replication was run.

Best-of-K, union-of-K and panel aggregation over critics therefore buy coverage in proportion to
how much of the population sits near the critic's boundary, and only to the extent that the route
is sampled at all. An auditor tuned loud enough, or sampled deterministically, is invariant under
resampling, and its union over K readings can rise only as far as its marginal cases allow.

\subsection{What the ceiling is made of}

Repetition is the smallest of the contributions we could measure. Eight readings of one auditor buy
19.3 points of union recall, and twenty readings across three families leave 51.8\% of the defect
population untouched. Changing which model reads moves the same quantity --- union recall at $K=8$
over the same 110 instances --- by 26.4 points, though \S\ref{sec:auditor} gives three reasons not
to read that as auditing ability. Changing one sentence of the rulebook moves flags by 26.8 points,
but on 56 instances at one reading, and we do not place that number on the same scale as the other
two.

A fourth contribution is not the auditor's at all, and we can only describe it post hoc. Under a
rule that asks whether the specification's prose determines the hidden suite's expected value, 44
of the 57 never-flagged defects fail that test. A constructed population where the prose does
determine the answer is audited at 84.2\% [73.8, 93.1] by the same auditor (\emph{in review},
\S\ref{sec:inreview}). The association between never being flagged and never being determined is
partly definitional, because the residual is defined by conditioning on never being flagged, so
this is an observation about where the misses sit and not an estimate of how much the benchmark
contributes.

The constructed population carries a limitation we measured rather than assumed. Its defects are
detectably artificial: a frontier model separates them from natural ones at 96.7\%. What the
construction licenses is a bound, that the measured ceiling lies above what an auditor can do on
defects the specification settles, and it leaves open how often natural defects of that kind are
caught.

\subsection{Flagging is not naming}

An audit that blocks an increment for the wrong reason has still blocked it, and a recall number
counts that as a catch. With the finding texts kept for one reading of the shipped auditor and
adjudicated blind by two raters, the auditor returned a finding on 9 of 110 defective instances
and asserted the actual failing behaviour on 5 to 7 of them, depending on how the two raters are
combined. The raters agreed at $\kappa$ = 0.391, which we report beside the rate it produces, because a
moderate agreement is part of what the rate is worth. That study's preregistered kill condition
fired on this result, and \S\ref{sec:inreview} states its headline in the form the kill requires.
The figure is a property of one reading and is not a naming rate for the eight-reading union;
establishing that would take adjudicating eight readings' texts, which were not archived.

\subsection{Limits}
\label{sec:limits}

The confound we would fix first is that auditor families are not sampled alike: one route is sent
temperature $0$ and the other no sampling parameter at all, which bounds how far one union can
grow before any difference in ability is measured. Every cross-family union-of-K contrast here
inherits it. Second, no auditor is compared with another at a matched false-positive rate; we have
two severity thresholds per family and no sweep between them, so the paper cannot say whether the
families differ once brought to the same operating point. Third, the primary intervals are
problem-cluster percentile bootstraps over 56 clusters whose coverage under this design has not
been validated by simulation, and no sample size was justified in advance by a detectable-effect
calculation. Fourth, both substrates are short, self-contained Python functions with executable
hidden tests, so nothing here measures auditing a change to a large program with no oracle, and
both are drawn from benchmarks old enough that memorisation cannot be ruled out. Fifth, the
generator is a single model on both substrates, so the defect population is one model's failure
mode. Sixth, the residual rubric's raters were a model and the author, who wrote the rubric and was
not blind to the earlier labels; one external label set agrees with them across eleven overlapping
tasks. Seventh, the injected population's defects are distinguishable from natural ones. Each of
these is recorded in the study that carries it, together with the number it would change.

\subsection{What we would do next}

A severity-threshold sweep within one substrate, producing a recall--false-positive curve for each
auditor family, is the experiment this paper most needs and the cheapest of these to run, because
the findings are already archived and only the grading rule changes. It would decide whether the
auditor contrast survives at a matched operating point. Replicating the cross-family arms with the
sampling parameters matched would remove the confound above, and extending the cross-vendor family
past eight readings would turn a statement about our budget into a statement about a curve. Beyond
that: adjudicating more than one reading of the shipped auditor would let a naming rate be compared
with a union flag rate at the same K; a second generator would show whether the residual's
character is a property of the benchmark or of one model's failures; a population of natural
defects independently known to be specification-determined would separate the injected population's
artificiality from the property it was built to isolate; and a substrate whose ground truth is not
a hidden test suite would test the assumption every ceiling here rests on, which is that a test
suite asked the right question.
