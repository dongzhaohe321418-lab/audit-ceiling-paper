\section{Related work in full}
\label{sec:related-full}

\textbf{Critics that read code.} Trained critic models write natural-language critiques of
model-written code and catch more bugs than unaided humans \citep{mcaleese2024critics}. That work
evaluates on both deliberately inserted and naturally occurring bugs. On code containing natural
model errors its critiques are preferred to human critiques in 63\% of cases, and it surfaces
errors in training data previously rated flawless. It also reports that critics hallucinate bugs,
and that a human--model team hallucinates less than the model alone. The natural-defect setting
and the precision cost of a louder critic are therefore already its territory, and we claim
neither. Its reported quantity is a preference or a per-critique comparison. What we add on this
axis is narrow: the union of repeated independent readings as an instrument for locating a limit,
ground truth supplied by an interpreter, and the false-positive rate carried beside every recall
figure. Aggregating several review passes is known to help. On 1{,}000 verified pull requests,
multi-review aggregation raises F1 by up to 43.67\% \citep{zeng2025swrbench}. The motivation is
shared with our union-of-$K$, but there an additional model call synthesises the sampled reviews,
while our union counts an instance whenever any reading flags it, so the two estimate different
things.

\textbf{Repeated sampling and coverage.} Coverage, the fraction of problems solved by at least one
of $K$ samples, rises steeply and then flattens. This is an established regularity of repeated
sampling, reported over four orders of magnitude of $K$ and fitted by a power law
\citep{brown2024monkeys}. Our union-of-$K$ is that quantity with a flag rule in place of a
correctness check, so \S\ref{sec:repeat} re-measures a known shape, and at $K \le 8$ our budget is
small beside theirs. Our contribution is the reading: the curve is taken as a measurement of a
ceiling, reported beside the false-positive rate it costs, and tested against a registered
flattening bar that it fails, which is why we report diminishing returns and no limit.

\textbf{Self-assessment.} Two lines of work concern a model judging its own output. Self-correction
without external feedback does not reliably improve reasoning and can degrade it
\citep{huang2024selfcorrect}. Models used as judges prefer their own generations, mostly
legitimately, since stronger generators are better judges, but harmfully on the instances they
themselves get wrong \citep{chen2025selfpreference}. Our same-vendor arms are the code-audit case of
that literature. Because the routes differ in model, operating point and sampling, we report their
contrasts as contrasts between routes, with the false-positive rate beside the recall.

\textbf{What the hidden suite decides.} EvalPlus rebuilt HumanEval and MBPP with larger suites and
reported defects in the original reference solutions \citep{liu2023evalplus}; its code carries
bespoke oracles for tasks where differential testing against the reference did not apply.
\citet{richter2026collapse} show that models collapse onto a single incorrect reading of an
underspecified task, and name twelve MBPP tasks they identified by hand. Eleven fall inside our
defect population, and our raters, who labelled them before that paper was fetched, agree on nine.
On the test-generation side, the dominant error in generated tests is a wrong expected value
rather than an invalid input \citep{xiong2023testing}, and generated oracles tend to capture what
a program does rather than what was intended \citep{konstantinou2024oracles}. Our claim is the
auditing counterpart: where the prose does not fix the value, a recall number computed against
that suite measures the benchmark as much as the auditor.

\textbf{Consensus as a substitute for ground truth.} Several systems validate generated tests
without a reference by agreement, through self-consistency, dual execution, or multi-agent
discussion for {JUnit} suites \citep{taherkhani2026consistency, xu2025consensus}. Both measure what
survives the filter against a ground-truth implementation: 84--91\% of retained tests valid in one
\citep{taherkhani2026consistency}, 0.89--0.93 oracle correctness in the other
\citep{xu2025consensus}. Our measurement differs in unit and in bar. It is the share of wrong tests
among retained \emph{failing test--candidate applications}, judged against a preregistered 2\%
threshold. It is taken over a frozen set of generated tests and candidates, and that set includes
candidates already failing their visible tests. Corroboration across independent generations does not meet that validation criterion
(\S\ref{sec:inreview}), and wrong tests recur by problem. That recurrence is compatible with the
self-consistent errors, the same wrong answer produced again, that degrade the detection methods
\citet{tan2025consistent} evaluate. Our records hold indices, not assertions, so they
do not show that the recurring errors are the same one.

\textbf{Auditing scientific work.} On manuscripts, no model exceeds 21.1\% recall or 6.1\% precision
on errata-grade errors and repeated runs rarely rediscover the same errors \citep{son2025spot}; the
best models detect 46.7\% of real paper--code discrepancies \citep{baumgartner2026scicoqa}. An LLM
auditor of AI-scientist projects detects hand-labelled pitfalls far better with logs and code than
with the paper alone \citep{luo2025pitfalls}. Pattern-based linting targets methodology bugs in
scientific Python \citep{samsonau2026scicodelint}, cleaning agents find row-level errors in corrupted
tables and miss errors spanning rows \citep{bendinelli2025cleaning}, and simulation code can run and
still solve the wrong equations \citep{song2026moose}. SciCode supplies scientist-written tests
\citep{tian2024scicode}, and an expert audit of it found many specifications and tests that reject
correct solutions \citep{hu2026scicodeverified}. Reproduction benchmarks ask agents to recover
reported results \citep{siegel2024corebench}. On constructed statistical workflows, LLM reviewers
detect planted flaws almost always but flag 17--50\% of correct scripts \citep{chelate2026replicationtrap},
and Luo et al.\ already report both true- and false-positive rates. Scientific auditing and paired
operating points are therefore not new. What we add is this measurement programme applied to
scientific code, and the comparison of the LLM tier with deterministic verification on reported
results and data, which we did not find in prior work.

\textbf{Who audits whom.} That an evaluator should not be the model under evaluation is supported
by measurements of self-preference in LLM judges against verifiable ground truth
\citep{chen2025selfpreference}. We do not measure self-preference. A frozen generator's code
audited by routes that differ in model, operating point and sampling cannot separate preference
from performance, so we report the same-vendor contrast as a contrast between routes, on one
substrate.

\textbf{Preregistration.} Preregistering an evaluation protocol is beginning to appear in machine
learning, both as a protocol for experiments with agents \citep{vaccaro2026prereg} and as a defence
against tuning prompts, decoding parameters or output format until a desired result appears
\citep{thomas2026phacking}. We count our use of it as method, not contribution.
